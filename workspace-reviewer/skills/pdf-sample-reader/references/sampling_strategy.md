# Sampling Strategy for Journal Sample PDFs

Use this guide when reading target-journal sample papers.

The purpose is not full paper summarization.
The purpose is to extract stable style signals that help manuscript review.

---

## 1. Start from a concrete question

Before opening PDFs, define what you need to learn.

Good questions:
- How are contributions usually framed in the Introduction?
- Are abstracts quantitative or mostly descriptive?
- Are figure captions long and self-contained?
- How detailed are methods sections?
- Are conclusions cautious or bold?

Bad questions:
- What does this whole paper say?
- Can I summarize every section of every sample paper?

---

## 2. Sample a few papers first

Default starting point:
- read 2 to 4 representative sample papers

Do not read all samples immediately.

Add more papers only when:
- patterns are inconsistent
- the current question is still unresolved
- topic-specific style differences may matter

---

## 3. Prefer representative samples

Prioritize papers that are:
- close to the manuscript topic
- close to the manuscript method type
- structurally complete and typical
- recent enough to reflect current venue style

Avoid relying too heavily on:
- very unusual papers
- survey papers unless the manuscript is also a survey
- papers with obviously atypical structure

---

## 4. Read by section priority

Use this order:

### First pass
- title
- abstract
- section headings
- introduction opening and closing paragraphs
- conclusion

### Second pass
- methods section opening
- experiment section opening
- figure captions
- table captions

### Third pass, only if needed
- selected detailed paragraphs
- equation presentation
- discussion or limitation sections

This keeps review efficient.

---

## 5. Extract style signals, not content summaries

For each sample, note patterns such as:
- common section sequence
- whether contributions are listed explicitly
- whether numerical results appear in abstract
- tone of claims
- citation density
- method detail level
- number and type of figures
- caption style
- result narration style
- conclusion restraint

Do not spend effort on paper-specific technical details unless directly relevant to style.

---

## 6. Compare across samples

After reviewing a few samples, ask:
- What patterns repeat?
- What seems stable?
- What varies by topic?
- What should the reviewer treat as a likely venue expectation?

Only repeated patterns should strongly influence judgment.

---

## 7. Update journal_style.md conservatively

When updating `journal_style.md`:
- write stable observations
- distinguish likely pattern from uncertain inference
- avoid absolute claims unless clearly supported
- keep notes short and reusable

Good:
- Introductions often end with an explicit contribution statement.
- Figure captions tend to be fairly self-contained.

Bad:
- The journal always requires exactly three contribution bullets.
- Every paper must include a separate Related Work section.

---

## 8. Use observations for review implications

Translate observations into review expectations.

Examples:
- If sample papers use self-contained captions, weak captions should be flagged.
- If sample papers use cautious conclusions, overclaiming should be flagged.
- If sample papers provide dense experimental comparison, weak baseline discussion should be flagged.

This is the main purpose of reading sample PDFs.

---

## 9. Escalate only when needed

If text extraction is poor or incomplete:
- inspect rendered page appearance
- check page layout, figure density, and caption structure visually
- focus on style evidence, not perfect extraction

Do not let low-quality extraction block useful style inference.
